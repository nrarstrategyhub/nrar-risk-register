I want to raise a risk — what do I do?

Four steps: prepare, go to the register, fill it in, then hand it over. This page tells you exactly where to go, what you'll need, and what happens after you lodge it.

1

Work out where your risk fits — before you open the register

The register asks you to classify the risk against NRAR's 12-category taxonomy. Picking the right category up front keeps the data clean and makes sure your risk lands in the right register tier.

Use the Category Explorer to find the category that fits, and look at the named risks listed under it — if one sounds like what you've spotted, you're in the right place. Check the risk appetite for that category too, so you know roughly how seriously it will be treated.

2

Go to the Risk Register → Operational Register tab → Add Risk

Most staff will raise risks in the Operational Register. If you're raising a risk that is clearly strategic or material — affecting the whole organisation, a major programme or a Board-level issue — it may go directly to the Enterprise Risk Register, but check with your manager first.

Not sure which register? Default to the Operational Register. The Risk & Assurance function will escalate to the Enterprise Register if the risk warrants it after assessment.
📊 Open Risk Register (Operational) → 🏛️ Enterprise Risk Register →

Links open the Risk Register. Navigate to the relevant register tab and click Add Risk.

3

What you'll be asked to fill in

You don't need to complete the full risk record — just the initial lodgement fields. A risk owner and the Risk & Assurance function will complete the rest with you.

4

Submit and hand it over — here's what happens next

Once you've lodged it, the process moves to the risk owner and the Risk & Assurance function. Your job is done unless you're named as a control owner.

1

Risk owner assigned

A manager or senior leader is named as owner — accountable for managing the risk

2

Controls identified

Existing controls are listed and rated for effectiveness (design × implementation)

3

Residual risk rated

Risk is re-rated after controls on the 5×5 grid — this is the number that matters

4

Response triggered

Medium → monitor. High/Critical → escalate, treatment plan, Executive visibility

What's my role? — the three questions everyone asks

RMF · §7
Am I the risk owner?

Usually no — unless you're a manager or executive

The risk owner is the person accountable for managing the risk. That's typically the manager responsible for the area where the risk sits, not the person who spotted it. If you're not sure, your manager can confirm. Raising a risk doesn't make you its owner.

Am I a control owner?

Possibly — if you operate a specific control

A control owner is accountable for a specific control being in place and working — for example, the person who runs a daily meter check, maintains a checklist, or manages a system. If you personally operate a control that reduces this risk, you may be named as its owner. Your manager will confirm.

What's my role after I raise it?

Reporter → then as directed

You've done the most important thing: you surfaced the risk. After lodging it, your role is to respond to follow-up questions from the risk owner or Risk & Assurance. If you're named as a control owner, you'll also be asked to evidence that your control is operating.

Frequently asked questions

What if I'm not sure it's a real risk — should I still raise it? ▶
Yes. If something is bothering you professionally, raise it. The assessment process will determine whether it's material and how to rate it. The cost of raising a risk that turns out to be minor is very low. The cost of not raising one that was real is much higher. When in doubt, raise it.
What's the difference between inherent and residual risk — and which do I fill in? ▶
When you first raise a risk, you fill in the inherent rating — how bad could it be with no controls in place at all. This is the baseline. The risk owner and Risk & Assurance will then assess the residual rating — the level after your existing controls are taken into account. Residual is the number that's compared to appetite and triggers the response. You don't need to work out the residual yourself.
I raised a risk months ago. What happened to it? ▶
Check the relevant register tab in the Risk Register — find your risk and look at its current residual rating, status and any treatment actions. If you can't find it or nothing seems to have happened, follow up with your manager or the Risk & Assurance function. The framework requires that risk records are kept current — a stale entry should be flagged.
What if the risk is about my team, my manager, or something sensitive? ▶
Raise it. Category 6 (Independence & integrity) exists precisely for risks involving conflicts of interest, capture or internal conduct. If you're not comfortable raising it through the normal register process, speak to the Risk & Assurance function directly, or use NRAR's speak-up and integrity reporting channels. Risk management and integrity reporting are complementary — not competing.
My risk is a project risk — does it go in the same place? ▶
Project risks go in the project risk register for that project, not the operational register. If a project risk is material — High or Critical residual, or likely to affect the broader organisation — it should also be flagged to the relevant Tier-2 or Enterprise register. Check with your project manager about what's already in place.
How do I rate the likelihood and consequence if I've never done it before? ▶
Use Module 03 in this hub — it walks you through both tables with the real descriptors. For consequence, read across all 7 domains and pick the worst one. For likelihood, think about how often this kind of event actually happens in practice given any controls already in place. If you're still unsure, make your best estimate and note your reasoning — the risk owner will review it.

The non-negotiables

Six things that apply to everyone, without exception

These minimum requirements apply to all staff and all activities. RMF · How to use this framework

1

Every material decision is risk-informed

Before any significant regulatory, operational or resource decision, a risk assessment is completed. The decision cannot proceed without it.

2

Every risk has a named owner

Accountability sits with a person — not a team, and not the risk function.

3

Risk is assessed consistently

All risks are rated on a residual basis using the same likelihood and consequence criteria.

4

High and Critical risks are escalated promptly

High and Critical residual risks, and any appetite breach, are made visible to the Executive and Board.

5

Controls relied on are evidenced

A control cannot be relied upon unless there is evidence it is well designed and operating in practice.

6

Risk records are kept current

Assessments, controls, treatments and acceptances are documented and kept up to date.

01

Risk fundamentals & key terms

The language the whole framework is built on

By the end of this module you can
  • Define risk as the effect of uncertainty on objectives — threats and opportunities
  • Tell inherent, residual and target residual risk apart, and say which one we actually manage
  • Explain what a control is and what "control effectiveness" means
  • Recognise the difference between appetite, tolerance and informed acceptance

What risk means here

RMF · Key terms

Risk is the effect of uncertainty on objectives — including both threats and opportunities. That second half matters: NRAR does not only manage things that might go wrong. Innovation and improvement are treated as an opportunity lens applied across every risk category, not a category of their own.

Because NRAR's work is inherently serious, risk management is not about eliminating risk. It is about taking managed risk where that is necessary to achieve lawful, fair, effective and efficient regulation — and being deliberate about it.

The three levels of a risk — flip each card

RMF · Key terms · §11.4

These three ratings are recorded for every material risk. Tap a card to reveal the meaning.

Inherent risk
Tap to reveal

The level of risk before controls are considered. Used as a baseline, so you can see how much the controls actually remove.

Residual risk
Tap to reveal

The level after existing, effective controls. This is what we actually manage, and what appetite is tested against.

Target residual risk
Tap to reveal

The lowest residual achievable with proportionate, reasonable controls. For safety risks, the level required by the WHS Act (so far as is reasonably practicable). We manage risks toward target.

Control
Tap to reveal

An action, process, system or behaviour that prevents, detects or corrects a risk. Control effectiveness is whether it is well designed and operating as intended in practice.

The one idea to hold onto: we manage the residual risk, we compare it to appetite, and we push it toward its achievable target. Where residual already equals target, the risk is being managed as well as it reasonably can be — even if that target is Medium.

What each rating lets you rely on

Appendix D.1
RatingRelianceMeaning
EffectiveFullVery strong design AND very strong or strong implementation. Controls consistently operating as intended.
Substantially effectiveHighStrong design with strong or adequate implementation. Mature and unlikely to fail significantly.
Partially effectivePartialAdequate design but limited or inconsistent implementation. Failures have occurred or are expected.
LimitedLowLimited design — reduces some aspects of risk but likely to fail. Heavy reliance on manual workarounds.

IneffectiveNoneConsistently not operating as intended. Rates of failure are significant and unacceptable.
The reliance rule. Only controls rated Effective or Substantially effective justify a reduction from inherent to residual. Anything lower is a control gap that needs treatment — or, where the residual is above target and can't be reduced at proportionate cost, documented informed acceptance. A control that doesn't exist yet is flagged "Not implemented" and gets no credit — it's a status, not an effectiveness rating.
05

Risk appetite & tolerance

A posture, not a ceiling

By the end of this module you can
  • Explain why appetite is a posture, not a maximum residual rating
  • Separate three ideas that are easy to confuse: appetite, tolerance and target
  • Apply the operating rule for "within appetite" vs "needs treatment"
  • Look up the required response for each residual rating

Why "above appetite" doesn't mean "controls failed"

RMF · §8.3

Because NRAR's work is inherently serious, the best residual most risks can reach — even with strong, fully effective controls — is Medium. A low appetite does not mean every residual must be Low. To avoid confusing "residual above appetite" with "controls have failed", the framework keeps three ideas separate:

Appetite

The posture — how much risk we'll take, and the outcomes we won't tolerate. Drives control strength, oversight intensity, escalation readiness. Not a residual ceiling.

Tolerance

The response bands — the residual ratings that mandate a response. Drives the accept / treat / escalate decision.

Target residual

What good looks like — the lowest residual achievable with proportionate controls. Residual is measured against this, not a fixed number.

Within appetite when…

§8.3 operating rule

All three hold:

✔ residual is within tolerance (Medium or below)
✔ controls are effective and operating
✔ residual is at or near its achievable target

A Medium residual with effective controls already at their practical floor is within appetite and needs monitoring, not treatment — even where category appetite is Low.

Treat or escalate when…

§8.3

Any one is true:
• residual is High or Critical (tolerance breach)
• controls are ineffective or not operating (control gap)
• residual is above its achievable target (a proportionate improvement exists)
• a specific intolerable outcome in a very-low-appetite area is exposed

06

Treatment & informed acceptance

Choosing a response — and accepting risk properly

By the end of this module you can
  • Pick from the six treatment options
  • Recognise when treatment is required
  • Run an informed acceptance correctly — and know who can accept at each level

The six treatment options

Appendix E.1
OptionWhen to use it
AvoidDecide not to start, or to stop, the activity that gives rise to the risk.
Reduce likelihoodStrengthen preventive controls so the event is less likely.
Reduce consequenceStrengthen detective or corrective controls so the impact is smaller.
Share or transferUse insurance, contracts or partnerships to share the risk — noting accountability cannot be transferred.
Retain (informed acceptance)Consciously accept the residual where it cannot be reduced further at proportionate cost.
Take or increaseAccept more risk to pursue a worthwhile opportunity, consistent with opportunity appetite.

Informed acceptance is a governed outcome — not a control failure

Appendix E.3

Where a residual sits above the appetite posture and can't be reduced further without disproportionate cost, it may be consciously accepted. To be valid, an acceptance must:

• be made by a person with the appropriate delegation
• record the basis, including why further treatment is not proportionate
• be visible to the Executive, and to the Board for material risks
• be time-bound, with a review date

A Medium residual with effective controls already at their practical floor is within appetite and is accepted as a matter of course.

One taxonomy, used everywhere

RMF · §9

NRAR uses one set of twelve categories to classify every risk, set appetite, and group controls. Using a single taxonomy is what lets risks be compared and aggregated across the organisation — from an activity-level assessment up to the Board. Innovation and change are an opportunity lens across all twelve, not a thirteenth category.

08

Special cases: AI & cultural values

When the control is an existing external instrument — not an NRAR process

By the end of this module you can
  • Recognise the small number of risks where NRAR uses an existing external instrument rather than authoring its own process
  • Name the instrument for cultural-values engagement and for AI use
  • Say what "Effective" control looks like in each case
The principle. For a few risks, the appropriate control is not an NRAR-authored process but adherence to an existing, externally-developed instrument. Where this applies, NRAR does not create a parallel or competing standard — it names the instrument to use and defines what "Effective" means in that context. Appendix G — intro

Cultural values & First Nations water rights

Category 4 · Appendix G.4

Cultural values are not a subset of environmental values — they are a distinct dimension of harm, with their own significance, knowledge holders and legal context (including the Water Management Act 2000 and native title). For a NSW regulator this is strategically material, not peripheral.

Instrument to use: NRAR engages through existing instruments — principally the engagement protocol developed by the Murray Lower Darling Rivers Indigenous Nations (MLDRIN), and the relevant DCCEEW Aboriginal Regional Water Strategies for the catchments NRAR operates in. Risk owners should obtain the current document before engaging, rather than designing a parallel NRAR process.

EffectivenessWhat it looks like here
EffectiveEngagement followed the applicable MLDRIN protocol or Regional Water Strategy, and adherence is evidenced (who was consulted, when, and how cultural input shaped the decision).
Partially effectiveSome steps followed but not evidenced, or engagement occurred but did not follow the agreed protocol.
IneffectiveNo recognised protocol was followed.

Artificial intelligence & automated decision support

Category 8 · Appendix G.8

A decision informed by an unexplainable or unvalidated automated output is a decision NRAR may not be able to defend on judicial review — and a biased or poorly governed tool can quietly embed unfairness into enforcement patterns long before it's detected. The framework names this as a distinct risk now, rather than waiting for an AI-related incident to force the issue.

Instrument to use: the NSW AI Assessment Framework (AIAF) — the NSW Government's mandatory, risk-based self-assessment for the design, development, deployment, procurement and use of AI, issued under DCS-2024-04 and aligned to the mandatory NSW AI Ethics Principles. It applies across the full AI lifecycle and produces its own residual rating. Where the AIAF rates residual risk high or critical, the assessment goes to the NSW AI Review Committee. Projects over $5M or funded by the Digital Restart Fund get additional oversight under the NSW Digital Assurance Framework.

EffectivenessWhat it looks like here
EffectiveAn AIAF has been completed for the system, is current, its residual rating recorded, and — where high or critical — escalated to and reviewed by the AI Review Committee.
Partially effectiveAn AIAF exists but is stale, incomplete, or was not escalated when the rating required it.
IneffectiveNo AIAF completed for an AI system in active development, procurement or use.
Note. NRAR's own rating for this entry tracks whether the AIAF process is being followed and kept current — it does not re-score the AI system's technical risk, which is the AIAF's own output.
